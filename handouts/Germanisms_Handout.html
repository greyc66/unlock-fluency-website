Germanisms — Common Mistakes Germans Make in English
Category ❌  Common mistake ✅  Correct English 🧠  Why this happens
Tense confusion
(present simple vs. continuous)		 "I'm living in Berlin."
"I'm working as an accountant."		 "I live in Berlin."
"I work as an accountant."
Use present simple for permanent states, habits, and facts.		 German has no grammatical progressive form — both permanent and ongoing states use the same ending. Learners default to -ing because it looks like an active present.
Present perfect vs. simple past "I have seen him yesterday."
"I have been there last year."		 "I saw him yesterday."
"I went there last year."
With a definite time marker, always use the simple past.		 In spoken German, the Perfekt is the default past tense for all completed events — recent or distant. Learners transfer this, unaware English requires simple past when a specific time is named.
since vs. for "I live here since five years."
"I'm waiting since an hour."		 "I have lived here for five years."
"I have been waiting for an hour."
Seit covers both "since" and "for" — English splits these into two words with different rules.		 German seit covers both "since a point in time" and "for a duration." Learners import the single German word and apply it to all contexts.
make vs. do "I make sport."
"She made her homework."		 "I do sport."
"She did her homework."
Do = activities and tasks. Make = creating or producing something.		 German has one verb — machen — covering both. Learners pick make (the more common dictionary match) and overgeneralise it across all contexts.
say vs. tell "He said me to come."
"She said me the truth."		 "He told me to come."
"She told me the truth."
Tell always takes a person. Say does not: say something (never say me).		 German sagen freely takes a person object (Er sagte mir). Learners transfer this pattern to say, unaware that English requires tell for a direct person object.
False friend:
bekommen		 "Can I become a receipt?"
"I became a letter from the bank."		 "Can I have a receipt?"
"I received a letter from the bank."
Bekommen = to receive. English become = to transform into something new.		 The brain retrieves bekommen when searching for become due to near-identical sound, then imports its German meaning ("to receive") into English.
False friend:
aktuell		 "Actually, I am working on a new project." (meaning: at the moment) "Currently, I am working on a new project."
Aktuell = current / currently. English actually = in fact / contrary to expectation.		 Both words share a Latin root but diverged in meaning. The phonological similarity causes the brain to treat them as equivalents when searching for words in real time.

Category ❌  Common mistake ✅  Correct English 🧠  Why this happens
False friend:
sympathisch		 "My new colleague is very sympathetic." (meaning: nice / likeable) "My new colleague is very nice / likeable."
Sympathisch = likeable. English sympathetic = showing compassion for someone suffering.		 Both words share the Greek root sympathia but evolved differently. German kept the "likeable" sense; English narrowed to "showing compassion toward someone suffering."
False friend:
sensibel		 "She is a very sensible person." (meaning: emotionally sensitive) "She is a very sensitive person."
Sensibel = emotionally sensitive. English sensible = practical and reasonable.		 Both derive from Latin sensibilis but shifted in opposite directions: German toward emotional sensitivity, English toward rational practicality. Same root, opposite meaning.
das Handy "I forgot my handy at home." "I forgot my phone / mobile at home."
In German, das Handy = mobile phone. In English, handy = convenient or useful (an adjective, never a noun for a phone).		 A pseudo-Anglicism: German borrowed the English adjective handy and repurposed it as a noun meaning mobile phone. Learners assume it works the same way in English.
über → talk about "We talked over the problem for hours." "We talked about the problem for hours."
Talk over exists in English but means to discuss in order to reach a decision — not to discuss generally.		 German über is a broad preposition. Learners transfer its "about" meaning but reach for the visually similar over rather than the correct about.
go home
(no preposition)		 "I'm going to home."
"She went to the home early."		 "I'm going home."
"She went home early."
English home as a destination is an adverb — it needs no preposition.		 German uses nach Hause (literally "to home"), making a preposition obligatory. Learners add "to" by direct transfer, not knowing English drops it entirely.
already vs. yet "I don't have the report already."
"Isn't she here already?"		 "I don't have the report yet."
"Isn't she here yet?"
Use yet in negatives and questions about completion. Already is for affirmative statements and surprised questions.		 German schon can appear in negative and question contexts where English requires yet. The distribution rules differ and must be explicitly learned.
werden → get / become "It becomes cold outside."
"I became very nervous before the meeting."		 "It's getting cold outside."
"I got very nervous before the meeting."
Use get for temporary changes of state, especially in spoken English.		 German werden covers both become (permanent transformation) and get (temporary change). Learners use become as the textbook translation, missing the idiomatic get.

Category ❌  Common mistake ✅  Correct English 🧠  Why this happens
Word order in subordinate clauses "I know that he tomorrow comes to the meeting."
"She said that she at six o'clock arrives."		 "I know that he is coming to the meeting tomorrow."
"She said that she arrives at six."
English word order in subordinate clauses is identical to main clause order (Subject – Verb – Object).		 German verb-final order in subordinate clauses (...dass er morgen kommt) is deeply embedded in L1 grammar. It surfaces automatically when building complex sentences in English.
müssen nicht → must not vs. don't have to "You must not worry about it." (meaning: there is no need to worry)
"You must not bring anything." (meaning: it isn't necessary)		 "You don't have to worry about it."
"You don't have to bring anything."
Must not = it is forbidden. Don't have to = it is not necessary. These are opposites.		 Nicht müssen = no obligation. Word-for-word translation gives "must not," which in English signals prohibition — the exact opposite of the intended meaning. One of the most dangerous German errors.
machen → take a photo "I made a photo of the Eiffel Tower."
"Can you make a photo of us?"		 "I took a photo of the Eiffel Tower."
"Can you take a photo of us?"
In English, you always take a photo — never make one.		 Ein Foto machen is the German collocation. Learners transfer make as the closest match for machen, unaware that English pairs take with photographs as a fixed chunk.
Definite article with proper nouns "I work at the BMW."
"The London is beautiful."
"The Professor Schmidt is here."		 "I work at BMW."
"London is beautiful."
"Professor Schmidt is here."
Names of companies, cities, and people do not take the in English.		 Some German dialects require articles with proper nouns (die BMW, die Müller). Learners apply this convention to English, where proper nouns almost always stand alone.
Reflexive verbs "I feel me very tired today."
"She sat herself down and relaxed."
"I remember me the conversation clearly."		 "I feel very tired today."
"She sat down and relaxed."
"I remember the conversation clearly."
Many verbs that are reflexive in German have no reflexive pronoun in English.		 German reflexive verbs (sich fühlen, sich setzen, sich erinnern) require a pronoun. In English, many equivalents are intransitive — the pronoun is simply absent, a rule requiring explicit instruction.
False friend:
eventuell		 "Eventually I could attend the meeting." (meaning: perhaps / maybe)
"Eventually we can find a solution." (meaning: possibly)		 "Possibly I could attend." / "I might be able to attend."
"Perhaps we can find a solution."
Eventually = after a long time / in the end — not "perhaps."		 Eventuell (= possibly) closely resembles eventually, triggering false retrieval. In English, eventually refers to something that happens after a long process — often the opposite of the intended meaning.

Germanisms — Answer Keys

Common Mistakes Germans Make in English  ·  The Unlock Fluency Method  ·  Dr Christina Grey
🔑  Answer Key — Exercise 1: Spot and correct the mistake
  1. "I'm living in London for three years."
    ✅ "I have lived in London for three years." — Use the present perfect for a state that started in the past and continues now. Use for with a duration.

  2. "Can you give me some informations about the course?"
    ✅ "Can you give me some information about the course?" — Information is uncountable in English. It has no plural form (same for advice, feedback, progress).

  3. "She said me the meeting was cancelled."
    ✅ "She told me the meeting was cancelled." — Tell takes a person object directly. Say does not: you say something, never say me.

  4. Correct answer: (b) "I saw that film last night."
    "Last night" is a definite past time marker. English requires the simple past with such markers. The present perfect I have seen cannot be used when a specific past time is named.

  5. "He became a very good mark in his exam."
    ✅ "He got a very good mark in his exam." — Bekommen is a false friend meaning "to receive / to get." English become means to transform into something new.

  6. "It's a beautiful city, not?"
    ✅ "It's a beautiful city, isn't it?" — English question tags use a reversed auxiliary + pronoun. The tag is negative when the main clause is positive.

  7. False. Actually = in fact / contrary to what you might think (German: eigentlich). Currently = at this moment in time (German: aktuell). They are false friends with entirely different meanings.
